Let the real world lead.

The kit comes first

Wonder Why Kit is first and foremost a physical kit. Carefully chosen materials invite children to touch, move, build, share and explore. Most of their time should be spent with those materials and the world around them.

Start with the object and follow the child’s interest. Leave space for repetition, surprises and their own ways of playing. The website is an optional companion: use it briefly when a closer look or a comparison helps, then return to hands-on play.

Helicopter Seeds: a companion guide

Audience: ages 8–12. Optional companion session: 15–25 minutes, adapted to children’s interest. Priority: engagement, agency, then understanding. Let learners play before asking them to explain.

The invitation

This is a Wonder Why Kit activity: Open. Play. Wonder. Give children room to enjoy the world and follow their curiosity. Avoid introducing it as a science lesson or an experiment. The concepts below support tutors; introduce explanations and vocabulary when children’s observations make them useful.

The central question

Why do maple seeds spin like helicopters, and how can that help a new tree grow?

The useful chain is: an asymmetric winged shape turns while falling → aerodynamic forces slow descent → wind has more time to carry the seed → dispersal can take it away from the parent's shade and competition. A winged maple fruit is called a samara; introduce that word after children have something to describe.

Autorotation is rotation powered by the movement through air, rather than a motor. Do not explain the swept circle as a solid umbrella: the moving wing interacts with the air, but does not become a solid disc.

Using the optional browser companion

  1. Play freely (3–5 minutes). Introduce Start round and the steppers. “What did you notice?” Allow laughter and attention to Peck and Dot.
  2. Make a prediction. “Which of these two seeds will stay in the air longer?” Use Drop pair – Light and heavy: wing size, release height and breeze are shared.
  3. Change one thing. Hold breeze and weight steady, change wing size, and compare several drops. Release locations are random, so a single landing is not proof.
  4. Slow down to look. At 0.1×, notice the wing and body turning. Return to 1× before comparing perceived durations. Playback speed changes observation time, not the intended physical rules.
  5. Connect to the tree. “What could a seed gain by landing away from its parent?” Discuss sunlight, water, space and other plants competing for them.
  6. Transfer beyond the screen. Compare a safely collected real seed or paper helicopter. Keep release height consistent. Children should not climb furniture to drop objects.

Questions that reveal thinking

  • With the same wing, what changed when the body became heavier?
  • What does the wind do that spinning alone does not?
  • Does a seed need to reach the farthest possible place to grow?
  • Could a seed land far away somewhere unsuitable?
  • How would you make the comparison fairer?
  • What evidence would change your prediction?

For ages 8–9, use “stays up longer”, “heavier”, “more wing” and oral predictions. For ages 10–12, introduce variables, repeated observations, variation, autorotation and inherited traits. These are facilitation suggestions, not claims of a validated curriculum programme.

Evolution without intention

The tree does not decide to invent a helicopter. Heritable variation exists across generations. Traits that contribute to successful reproduction can become more common. This game illustrates a possible benefit of dispersal; it does not simulate inheritance, populations, selection or the historical evolution of a species. Character feelings are storytelling, not explanations of evolution.

What the controls actually do

Control Interpretation
Breeze Changes wind strength; default 70%. Manual wind travels right and toward the meadow.
Wing size Changes wing appearance and the simplified descent rate.
Seed weight Changes body appearance and descent rate. Values are relative settings, not grams.
Playback speed 0.1× to 1× viewing speed; default 1×.
Drop pair Same wing and height, light and heavy bodies, slightly separated positions. Uses two seeds.
Autoplay Disables manual breeze/wing/weight controls. Alternates weight and wing comparisons and changes wind. Best for noticing patterns, not strict controlled comparisons.

The round automatically releases seeds; the drop buttons can add releases sooner. Ten total seeds are available. The score describes two outcomes, rather than a moral success/failure judgement.

Model limits to discuss

  • Descent is a qualitative formula, not a fluid simulation. Larger wings slow descent and heavier bodies speed it up within the chosen settings. Real seed performance also depends on shape, orientation, flexibility and airflow.
  • Spin is animated independently; the game does not calculate lift from the displayed wing rotation.
  • Wind is deliberately biased right for visibility and usually toward the meadow in autoplay. This is a composition choice, not a rule of nature.
  • The fence, chickens and immediate sprouting are narrative devices. Real germination takes time and depends on conditions; landing in a meadow is not a guarantee of survival.
  • Ground is simplified, paired seeds are slightly offset, and release points vary. Treat comparisons as observations of a model.
  • The recorded drop inspired the project. Its height and timing are approximate, and it does not validate this version's numerical physics.

Evidence of understanding

Ask the learner to explain a new prediction: “If two seeds have the same weight but different wings, what might happen, and why?” Look for a causal connection between time airborne and opportunity for dispersal, rather than memorised vocabulary or a high score. Follow up with a real-object comparison if possible.

Access and classroom use

No account or microphone is needed. Buttons work with keyboard focus and activation. Text explanations remain available if 3D cannot run. The visual simulation does not yet offer an equivalent nonvisual experience; pair learners, narrate events, and use real objects when useful. Reduced-motion preference reduces tree sway but does not stop all animation. See accessibility and privacy.
